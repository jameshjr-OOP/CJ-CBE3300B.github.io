Electrical Design Considerations

The initial circuit is the same one shown in the preliminary design report. It is made up of an IR LED and photodiode in the finger cuff, a transimpedance amplifier (TIA) to convert the photodiode current into a voltage while reducing noise, a high-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 0.57 Hz to remove the DC baseline, and a non-inverting amplifier with a gain of 10 to amplify the AC signal we care about. The video below is the output we got from this initial circuit on the Arduino serial plotter, which shows that we are picking up something that resembles a pulse waveform but with a fairly low signal range.

After looking at the output of the initial circuit, we identified that the signal is far too low - the entire pulse waveform is contained within roughly 4 digital counts on the Arduino's 10-bit ADC. The signal-to-noise ratio is decent, but the signal itself is so small that small changes in the environment or finger placement can easily drown it out. To address this, the adjusted circuit increases the non-inverting gain by raising the feedback resistor and adding a second op-amp stage. We are also considering using a brighter LED (or a second LED in parallel) so that more light reaches the photodiode, and implementing background subtraction by blinking the IR LED on and off and subtracting the off-state reading from the on-state reading to remove ambient light contributions.

Mechanical Design Considerations

The current "finger box" design is functional but has a few issues that we want to address in the next iteration. The photodiode and LED are not flush with the skin, which means there is a small air gap between the components and the finger that allows ambient light to reach the photodiode and reduces the consistency of the signal. The box is also not particularly ergonomic - it requires the user to hold their finger flat against an opening rather than letting the device conform to the finger.

The mechanical improvements we plan on making are to sink the photodiode into the casing for more stability, design the casing so that the photodiode and LED are flush with the skin, and overall make the design more ergonomic. The most significant change is moving from the box design to a finger clip design, which is the standard form factor for commercial pulse oximeters.

We also considered several options for ambient light isolation. The current design relies on the box itself blocking light, but this isn't fully effective. We considered a mesh covering, a fully enclosed design, an optical baffle, and a clip design. Each has tradeoffs between user friendliness and light isolation - the enclosed and clip designs offer the best ambient light isolation, while the mesh and optical baffle designs are more accommodating of different finger sizes. We are moving forward with the clip design because it gives us the best balance of light isolation, ergonomics, and consistency with commercial products.

Further Market Research and Cost Analysis

To better understand where our product would sit in the market, we looked at competitors that offer heart rate monitoring as part of their product. The smartwatch category (Apple, Samsung, Fitbit) typically offers heart rate, blood oxygen, sleep tracking, and other fitness features at a price range of roughly $65 to $799. The dedicated pulse oximeter category (Masimo, Medtronic, Wellue) offers heart rate and blood oxygen at a price range of roughly $20 to $700. Across all six competitors we looked at, the average price range is approximately $148 to $522.

Looking at the bill of materials for our own product, the component cost is significantly lower than what is on the market. The IR LED is roughly $0.90, the photodiode is $1.40, the resistors total $0.25, the capacitors total $0.21, the op-amps total $0.64, the microcontroller is between $5 and $15 depending on whether we use the Arduino Uno or a smaller microcontroller, and the mini LCD is roughly $5. The total component cost is between $13.40 and $23.40. Even after accounting for the 3D printed housing, assembly, and a reasonable markup, our product could be priced well below the competitor average while still maintaining margin. The tradeoff is that our product currently only measures heart rate, not blood oxygen, and is bulkier than a smartwatch.
